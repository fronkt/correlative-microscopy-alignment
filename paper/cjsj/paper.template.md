% Which Automatic Image Registrations Can a Microscopist Trust? A Label-Free Test on a Correlative Microscopy Benchmark
% Frank Cai
% Homestead High School, Fort Wayne, Indiana, USA

Abstract—Correlative microscopy combines images of one specimen taken with different instruments, which first requires registration: finding the transformation that maps one image onto the other. Foundation image matchers can register images automatically, but on the AmalgaMatch benchmark of {{n_pairs}} materials-science image pairs they succeed on only about a quarter, and they give no warning when they fail. I tested whether scores that need no ground truth can tell which registrations to trust. Each pair was registered {{n_voters}} ways, and three scores were compared: the retained fraction (the share of proposed point matches that survive robust fitting), the agreement between registrations, and a combination of the two. {{ABSTRACT_RESULTS}} The hypotheses and tests were fixed and published before the runs reported here.

# Introduction

Correlative microscopy places images of the same region of a specimen, taken with different instruments, on one coordinate grid, so that every position carries several kinds of measurement, for example crystal orientation from electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) and surface contrast from a scanning electron microscope (SEM) [1]. The first step is registration: finding the transformation that maps the image with the smaller field of view (here called the narrow image) into the one with the larger field of view (the wide image). Registration by hand is slow. Image matchers pretrained on large collections of photographs, such as RoMa [3] and its version retrained on images from different sensors, MatchAnything-RoMa [4], can do it automatically. On AmalgaMatch, a public benchmark of {{n_pairs}} correlative image pairs from materials science [1], [2], they register only about one pair in four to within 20 pixels [5].

These matchers also do not report failure. RoMa and MatchAnything-RoMa, as configured, return 10,000 point matches (correspondences: pairs of points believed to show the same feature) for every pair, whether or not the two images show the same place, and robust fitting always finds some that agree [5]. A researcher who runs automatic registration on a new batch of images therefore cannot tell which results to keep.

Durmaz et al. [2] found that the share of correspondences surviving robust fitting separated good from bad registrations on 48 pairs from four subsets of the benchmark, and proposed using it to choose between image pairs of different modalities and to send doubtful cases to a human. This paper tests that idea on the whole benchmark. **H1:** the retained fraction predicts success on all pairs, and a cut-off chosen on the kinds of pairs Durmaz et al. studied still works on the other kinds. **H2:** combining the retained fraction with the agreement between several registrations of the same pair predicts success better than the retained fraction alone. **H3:** extending their proposal from modality pairs to registration methods, choosing one registration per pair with these scores registers more pairs than the best single method. The hypotheses, statistics and pass criteria were committed to a public repository before the runs reported here [6]. A pilot on earlier runs of the same pairs, in which the number of surviving correspondences gave an area under the curve (defined below) of {{pilot_ma}} for MatchAnything-RoMa and {{pilot_roma}} for RoMa, motivated the design and the pass bar for H1.

# Methods

## Data
AmalgaMatch [1] contains {{n_pairs}} image pairs from {{n_scenes}} scenes, {{n_subclasses}} combinations of material and instruments, and six task groups (for example serial sectioning, fracture surfaces, and dislocation imaging in the transmission electron microscope, TEM). Every pair has hand-annotated corresponding points, used only to score results, never to produce them. The data are released under a CC BY 4.0 licence.

## Candidate registrations
Each pair was registered {{n_voters}} ways (the voting pool). Five matchers were applied to the whole images: the classical SIFT [7], LoFTR [8], RoMa [3], and the MatchAnything versions of RoMa and of Efficient LoFTR [4]. RoMa and MatchAnything-RoMa were also run inside a coarse-to-fine search that zooms into the wide image [5], and on four label-free transformations of the images: the narrow image with inverted contrast, the narrow image with its brightness histogram matched to the wide image, both images after local contrast equalization (CLAHE), and both images replaced by their gradient magnitude. As a control for random variation, RoMa and MatchAnything-RoMa were rerun with five further random seeds; these ten runs never vote. Each set of correspondences was fitted with the robust estimator MAGSAC++ [9] (5.5-pixel threshold), which repeatedly fits a transformation to small random subsets of correspondences and keeps the one that the most correspondences agree with (its inliers). The transformation is affine (shift, rotation, scale and shear) or a homography (which also allows perspective), following the benchmark protocol [1].

## Label-free scores
Three scores were computed without any annotated points. **S1**, the retained fraction [2], is the number of inliers divided by the number of correspondences proposed. **S2** measures agreement. For a candidate and each other member of the voting pool, both transformations are applied to a 5 × 5 grid of points spanning the narrow image, and the mean distance between the two mapped grids is taken. S2 is minus the median of these distances, so a candidate that lands where most others land scores high. **S3** averages the percentile ranks of S1 and S2, ranked over all pair and candidate combinations in the voting pool.

## Evaluation
A registration's error is the mean distance, in pixels of the wide image, between where it maps the annotated points and where they truly lie, with no refinement step. A registration succeeds if this error is at most 20 pixels. The best a single global transformation can do is estimated by fitting a homography to each pair's own annotated points. How well a score separates successes from failures is measured by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC): the probability that a randomly chosen success scores higher than a randomly chosen failure, where 0.5 is chance and 1 is perfect. Because pairs from the same scene share images, 95 % confidence intervals come from resampling whole scenes with replacement 10,000 times. Paired differences in success counts are tested with the exact McNemar test [10], which uses only the pairs on which two methods disagree. For the transfer test, a cut-off on S1 was chosen by Youden's index [11] (the cut-off that maximizes the share of successes accepted minus the share of failures accepted) on the two task groups Durmaz et al. studied, same-slice and serial sectioning, and applied unchanged to the other four. For triage, pairs are ranked by score and the highest-scoring ones accepted. Results outside the pre-registered list are labelled exploratory.
